Ultrasound Segmentation for Portable Devices

The problem

Bladder volume tells a care team whether a patient is retaining urine. The standard way to measure it is a catheter, which carries a risk of infection. Ultrasound can do the job without one, if a model can outline the bladder reliably and run on the small device at the bedside.

There is no public bladder ultrasound dataset, so the model had to learn from 486 images labeled for this project. A model that small devices can run also has to survive having its weights compressed, and that turned out to be the harder constraint.

The paper is joint work with Andrew Vu, Dr. Eric Chou and Dr. Gregory Thoreson of Baylor University Medical Center, and Dr. Katherine Brown at UT Dallas.

Building the labels

Outlining the bladder by hand on every frame is slow, so I built a pipeline that drafts the labels first. It asks SAMUS, an ultrasound adaptation of Segment Anything, for a bladder mask on each frame and cleans the mask up. A radiologist then graded the masks by hand to make sure they were accurate before any model trained on them.

What I built

I trained U-Net segmentation models with four encoders, from ResNet34 down to the small MobileNetV2 and EfficientNet-B0. Before they saw a bladder, I pretrained each encoder on 9,204 thyroid and breast ultrasound images from the public US30K collection, which contains no bladder images at all, and compared that against the usual ImageNet starting point.

To see what would survive on a small device, I quantized each trained model’s weights to 8 and 4 bits without retraining, using symmetric per-channel quantization, and scored it again on the 97 held-out images with the Dice score, the overlap between the predicted outline and the sonographer’s.

What it showed

At full precision the starting point barely matters. Every model lands between 95.4 and 96 percent Dice, and the best, a MobileNetV2 pretrained on ultrasound, reaches 96. At 8 bits nothing changes by more than a point. The difference appears at 4 bits.

Models that started from ImageNet fell apart, EfficientNet-B0 down to 11 percent. Models that started from ultrasound kept 75 to 93 percent. Pretraining on the right kind of image left full-precision accuracy where it was and made the models far more robust to compression, which is the property a bedside device needs.

Breast ultrasound, scored on unseen images

On the public BUSI breast ultrasound dataset I trained an attention U-Net and scored it on 156 test images that played no part in training or in choosing the checkpoint. Splitting the score by class shows where the error comes from.

Normal scans contain no lesion, and the model drew one anyway on more than half of them. Those false alarms account for a third of the Dice the model loses while making up a fifth of the test set, so the cheapest next gain is a better call on whether a lesion is there at all.

Next

Next is running the 4-bit models on the target hardware, where so far the compression is simulated, and checking predicted volumes against catheter measurements.
