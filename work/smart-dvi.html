Smart DVI

A Meta Quest 3 app that walks a technician through a vehicle inspection hands-free, and the AI layer I lead behind it.

Role
Lead engineer. I direct development and own the AI layer.
Status
In production at auto-repair shops
Stack
Unity (C#), Python, SQL, vision-language models, on-device speech recognition

The problem

A digital vehicle inspection is the checklist a technician works through on every car that comes into a shop. Each item gets a grade, anything wrong gets a photo, and the write-up goes to the service advisor, who uses it to explain the repair to the customer. Done on a tablet, it means stopping work to take photos and type with dirty hands, and the result depends on who did it.

What I built

Smart DVI moves the inspection onto a headset. The technician sees the shop’s own checklist in front of them, grades and photographs items without putting tools down, and dictates notes. Findings reach the advisor while the car is still on the lift. I lead the app’s development and own its AI layer, the part that turns photos, speech and the car’s own data into checklist answers.

The AI layer

Each kind of input gets its own handling, and in every case the technician has the last word.

I built those readings as small agents on the headset, which a shop switches on item by item. Each one wakes up when something happens in the inspection, such as a new photo, a voice note or a diagnostic session. The shop also decides whether an agent may fill in an answer or only suggest one, and an agent never overwrites what the technician entered. When an agent is unsure of a reading, it offers the value instead of writing it.

Reading the vehicle identification number shows the approach in small. The headset takes a burst of frames and keeps the sharpest, tries the barcode first, and falls back to a vision model that marks the characters it is unsure of. The number’s own check digit then repairs those characters, so a smudged plate still gives a valid number.

How I decide what ships

Changes to the AI start with measurement on real inspections. Before changing how photos are graded, I build an evaluation set from graded inspection photos and compare candidate models and prompts on it, and the result decides what the backend adopts. The same measurements decide where the AI may act alone and where it should only suggest, which is why photo grades stay suggestions the technician confirms.

Beyond the headset, I write the specifications the backend and web teams build against, citing the code behind each defect, and I spend time in the shop with the technicians using it.
